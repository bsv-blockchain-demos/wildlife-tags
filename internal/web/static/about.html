In one paragraph

SCDNR tags Atlantic blue crabs around Charleston and depends on crabbers to report the ones they catch. The science is sound and the field protocol works. What does not work is the incentive: the reward arrives weeks later, often to the wrong person, and by then it has stopped being an incentive at all. This replaces the printed phone number with a QR code. Scanning a tag pays the finder in seconds, and writes their position and measurements into the same transaction that moves the money — so the payment and the data cannot come apart.

The problem is not the tagging

Most tags are never reported

SCDNR's own review of reward tagging puts it plainly: fewer than half of the tags recreational anglers encounter are ever reported. The same review notes that tags carrying money report materially better than tags that do not.

The reward arrives too late to be one

The fisheries literature blames delivery, not generosity: the finder's identity is lost when a captain collects the tag, weeks pass, and the moment is gone. Today's offer is SCDNR swag or a chance at a $500 drawing — a lottery, not a payment.

Recapture positions are the weakest data you have

Release coordinates are precise, because a biologist recorded them on the spot. Recapture coordinates are remembered days later and typed into a form. For a study about movement, that asymmetry is exactly backwards.

The thing people actually want, nobody sends

SCDNR observed that many people who report a tag are more interested in the animal's history than the reward. One angler explained it simply: understanding his prey made him better at catching it. That information already exists and costs nothing to return.

The reward and the record are the same object

Arming a tag creates one Bitcoin output. Its value is the reward. Its locking script carries the tagging record. They are not two things that reference each other — they are one thing, and that is where most of the properties below come from.

<tag key> OP_CHECKSIGVERIFY <DNR key> OP_CHECKSIG
"WILDTAG" "2" <tag id> "ACT" <generation>
<observation> <signature> <observer key> <settlement>
OP_2DROP OP_2DROP OP_2DROP OP_2DROP OP_DROP ← the nine fields, pushed then discarded

The shape is BRC-48 “PushDrop”: a key check, then data pushed and immediately dropped. Dropping it is the point — the fields take no part in deciding whether a spend is valid, so they can be anything, and what they are is the record: where the animal was, what it measured, who says so, and their signature over it.

The record cannot be edited later

Not because anyone is trusted not to, but because the record is the script that locks the money. Change a coordinate and you have written a different script, which does not hold the coin, which nobody can spend. There is no version of this where the payment survives and the data quietly moves.

Reporting cannot be separated from being paid

The only way to release the reward is to spend that output, and spending it publishes the next record in the same transaction. A finder cannot take the money without filing the report, and DNR cannot lose the report while the money moves.

It pays exactly once

A coin is spent or it is not. A second claim on the same tag is a double-spend, and the network refuses it — not a database somewhere deciding it has seen this tag before. Nothing has to remember; the ledger already does.

Only the tag can start it

The first key is derived from the code printed inside the QR, which never leaves the phone that scans it. That signature can only come from something in physical contact with the tag. It says nothing about who or where — see below — but possession it does establish.

Why there is a second key, and what it costs

A tag's code is printed on something that never changes, so the person who reports a recapture holds exactly the code the next finder will hold. Without a second signature they could come back and take the next reward, and the bonus held in their own name along with it. No cleverness in key derivation fixes this: a past bearer can always re-derive whatever a future bearer derives.

So DNR co-signs. What that buys is replay protection across a tag's whole life, and the ability to refuse a claim on a tag reported stolen. What it costs is real: a finder cannot take the money unilaterally, which is a genuine reduction in how trustless this is, and we would rather write that down than let somebody discover it. What survives it: the finder's own signature still establishes, on its own, that they held the tag. Their report — the fix among it — is still bound to that signature, so it cannot be edited afterwards by us or by them; what the fix is worth is a separate question, answered honestly further down. And a refusal is publicly visible: the output simply stays unspent, where anyone can see it.

Why the record is in the output at all

A hash would have been cheaper. But a hash only proves a file has not changed, and somebody still has to keep the file — so the dataset lives exactly as long as the database holding it, which is the arrangement this was meant to replace. Putting the observation itself in the output means the public dataset can be rebuilt from the chain after every server involved is gone.

Paying for releases without taking anyone's word

A tagged crab put back in the water can produce another data point. A kept one cannot. So releasing is worth more — but nothing on a receipt can show that a crab really went back.

So the bonus is not paid on the reporter's word. It is held against the tag, committed to that reporter, and released only if the same crab is reported again. A second sighting is the closest thing to evidence a release can have. A false claim earns the base reward and forfeits a bonus that will never arrive.

  • Crabber reports a tag and puts the crab back → paid the base reward now.
  • Their bonus is locked to the tag, in their name.
  • Someone else catches the same crab later → they are paid, and the first reporter's bonus is released in the same transaction.

What it costs to try

The honest answer is: one batch of tags and an afternoon. Almost nothing about the existing programme has to change.

The tag is the same tag

Same 1×2 inch plastic stock, wired through the lateral spines exactly as now. A QR code is added; nothing is removed. Keep the phone number on it — a tag can carry both, and anyone who would have called still can.

The field protocol is unchanged

Still only mature hard-shell crabs, for the same reason as always: a peeler sheds the tag at its next moult and a soft-shell is harmed by handling. The console enforces it rather than relying on memory — and it reads that rule from the species file, so a different animal gets its own rules rather than the crab's.

It runs alongside, not instead

A pilot is one batch. Tags that carry a code participate; every other tag in the water behaves exactly as it does today. There is no cutover and nothing to roll back.

The data comes out as a CSV

One row per event, the columns a movement study already uses, plus the transaction id and whether its proof has been verified. The measurement columns follow whatever is actually in the data, so a fish export carries no empty carapace-width column. It opens in whatever you use now.

One binary, one config

A single static binary and a handful of environment variables. SQLite on a laptop or Postgres in a cluster. No accounts to provision and no vendor to sign with.

The budget is a number you choose

Rewards are set per tag and funded from one wallet, so the maximum spend of a pilot is decided before it starts. Rewards on tags never recaptured can be reclaimed after a set date — a real requirement, since crabs shed tags at every moult.

It is not a crab system

Blue crab is the pilot, not the product. Nothing underneath it — a bearer key printed on a tag, a one-time reward that can be claimed once, a timestamped record nobody can edit afterwards — is a fact about Callinectes sapidus.

A species is a file

What differs between animals is a list: which measurements are taken, which choices are recorded, and which sizes the law protects. That list is a JSON file the server publishes, and every form on every device is built from it. Adding red drum took no code at all — and red drum is deliberately awkward, with a slot limit rather than a minimum and no moult stage to record.

The same rules everywhere

The five-inch minimum and the egg-bearing-female rule are written once and enforced on the server, in the browser and on the phone from that one definition. A device that carried its own copy would eventually tell somebody they may keep an animal the server then refuses — after they have already killed it.

SCDNR already runs the harder case

The marine game fish programme covers 46 species across 20 families, tagged largely by volunteers rather than staff, with the same tag-release-recapture shape and the same reward problem. That is the same programme with a different list.

Harvest tagging is a different shape

Deer is not mark-recapture: one party, one event, compliance rather than reward. South Carolina already requires electronic reporting through SC Game Check, which issues a confirmation number a processor has to trust a database to validate. A one-time tag that anyone can check without asking us is exactly that, without the database. It is modelled here and not built — saying so is the point.

What we are not claiming

The same section appears on the page a crabber sees, because a programme that oversells its evidence is worse than one that has less of it.

When a record was made, and that it has not changed
Each tagging and each report is a Bitcoin transaction. Once a block carries it the record cannot be backdated or edited — not by a crabber, not by SCDNR, not by us.
That the reporter held the tag
The reward unlocks only with the key printed on the tag itself.
That each reward is paid once
It is a single coin. A duplicate claim fails.
Who stands behind a record
Every record is signed by the biologist or crabber who made it, and anyone reading the public dataset can re-derive the signing key and check it themselves.
Where anyone actually was
Positions come from the phone that scanned the tag and are taken on trust. The accuracy the device reports is recorded alongside every fix. This is an attestation, and we will not describe it as anything more.
That a crab was really released
Also taken on trust. The held bonus makes a false claim unprofitable rather than impossible, and a second sighting corroborates it. That is not the same as evidence.

Trying it

  • Create a batch and print the sheet on waterproof tag stock. Tag ids and codes are generated together; nothing is spent yet.
  • Fund one wallet with the pilot's budget.
  • In the field, scan a tag, record the crab as you already do, and arm it. The reward is locked to that tag at that moment.
  • A crabber scans it, reports the catch, and is paid. You get a position fix taken where they stood, not where they remembered standing.
  • Export the dataset whenever you like; check any row against the chain.
