About the programme

SCDNR tags Atlantic blue crabs around Charleston and relies on crabbers to report the ones they catch. The science and the field protocol both work.

What doesn't work is the incentive. Today's reward arrives weeks later, often to the wrong person — and by then it isn't one anymore.

This replaces the printed phone number with a QR code. Scanning a tag pays the finder in seconds, and writes their position and measurements into the same transaction that moves the money — so the payment and the data can't come apart.

Why more than half of today's tags go unreported, in SCDNR's own numbers.

Most tags are never reported

SCDNR's own review puts it plainly: fewer than half of the tags anglers encounter are ever reported. Tags carrying money report meaningfully better than tags that don't.

The reward arrives too late to be one

The fisheries literature blames delivery, not generosity: a captain collects the tag, the finder's identity gets lost, and weeks pass before anything arrives. Today's offer is swag, or a chance at a $500 drawing — a lottery, not a payment.

Recapture positions are the weakest data you have

Release coordinates are precise: a biologist recorded them on the spot. Recapture coordinates are remembered days later and typed into a form — backwards, for a study about movement.

The thing people actually want, nobody sends

SCDNR has found that many reporters care more about the animal's history than the reward — one angler put it simply: knowing his prey made him better at catching it. That information already exists and costs nothing to send back.

Arming a tag creates one Bitcoin output: its value is the reward, its locking script is the tagging record. They aren't two things referencing each other — they're one thing, and most of what follows comes from that.

<tag key> OP_CHECKSIGVERIFY <DNR key> OP_CHECKSIG
"WILDTAG" "2" <tag id> "ACT" <generation>
<observation> <signature> <observer key> <settlement>
OP_2DROP OP_2DROP OP_2DROP OP_2DROP OP_DROP ← the nine fields, pushed then discarded

The shape is BRC-48 “PushDrop”: a key check, then data pushed and immediately dropped. Dropping it is the point — the fields play no part in validating the spend, so they can be anything, and what they are is the record: where the animal was, what it measured, and who signed for it.

The record cannot be edited later

Not because anyone is trusted not to — the record is the script that locks the money. Change a coordinate and you've written a different script, one that doesn't hold the coin and that nobody can spend. The payment can't survive the data quietly moving.

Reporting cannot be separated from being paid

The only way to release the reward is to spend the output, and spending it publishes the next record in the same transaction. A finder can't take the money without filing the report.

It pays exactly once

A coin is spent or it isn't. A second claim is a double-spend, refused by the network — not by a database deciding it's seen this tag before. Nothing has to remember; the ledger already does.

Only the tag can start it

The first key is derived from the code printed inside the QR, which never leaves the phone that scans it — so that signature can only come from something in physical contact with the tag. It says nothing about who or where (see below), only that whoever holds the tag signed.

Why there is a second key, and what it costs

A tag's code never changes, so a past finder could re-derive the same key a future one holds. DNR co-signs to stop that — at a real cost, spelled out below.

A tag's code is printed on something that never changes, so whoever reports a recapture holds exactly the code the next finder will hold. Without a second signature, they could come back and take the next reward — plus the bonus already held in their name. No key-derivation trick fixes this: a past bearer can always re-derive whatever a future bearer derives.

So DNR co-signs. That buys replay protection across a tag's whole life, and the ability to refuse a claim on a tag reported stolen. The real cost: a finder can't take the money unilaterally, a genuine reduction in how trustless this is — we'd rather write that down than let someone discover it. What survives: the finder's own signature still proves they held the tag, and their report is still bound to it, unchangeable by anyone. A refusal is public too: the output just stays unspent, visible to all.

Why the record is in the output at all

A hash would have been cheaper, but a hash only proves a file hasn't changed — somebody still has to keep the file, which is the arrangement this was meant to replace. Putting the observation itself in the output means the dataset can be rebuilt from the chain after every server is gone.

How the re-release bonus is paid without trusting the reporter.

A released crab can produce another data point; a kept one can't. So releasing is worth more — but nothing on a receipt proves a crab really went back.

So the bonus isn't paid on the reporter's word. It's held against the tag, committed to that reporter, and released only if the same crab is reported again — the closest thing to evidence a release can have. A false claim earns the base reward and forfeits a bonus that never arrives.

  • Crabber reports a tag and puts the crab back → paid the base reward now.
  • Their bonus is locked to the tag, in their name.
  • Someone else catches the same crab later → they are paid, and the first reporter's bonus is released in the same transaction.

One batch of tags and an afternoon — what has to change, and what doesn't.

The honest answer is: one batch of tags and an afternoon. Almost nothing about the existing programme has to change.

The tag is the same tag

Same 1×2 inch plastic stock, wired through the lateral spines exactly as now — a QR code is added, nothing removed. Keep the phone number too: a tag can carry both, and anyone who'd have called still can.

The field protocol is unchanged

Still only mature hard-shell crabs — a peeler sheds the tag at its next moult, and handling harms a soft-shell. The console enforces this from the species file rather than relying on memory, so a different animal gets its own rules, not the crab's.

It runs alongside, not instead

A pilot is one batch: tagged-with-a-code tags participate, every other tag behaves exactly as today. No cutover, nothing to roll back.

The data comes out as a CSV

One row per event, the columns a movement study already uses, plus the transaction id and whether its proof is verified. Measurement columns follow the actual data, so a fish export carries no empty carapace-width column — and it opens in whatever you already use.

One binary, one config

A single static binary and a handful of environment variables — SQLite on a laptop, Postgres in a cluster. No accounts to provision, no vendor to sign with.

The budget is a number you choose

Rewards are set per tag and funded from one wallet, so a pilot's maximum spend is decided before it starts. Unclaimed rewards can be reclaimed after a set date — necessary, since crabs shed tags at every moult.

Blue crab is the pilot; the mechanism underneath fits any tag-and-recapture programme.

Blue crab is the pilot, not the product. Nothing underneath it — a bearer key on a tag, a one-time claimable reward, a timestamped record nobody can edit — is a fact about Callinectes sapidus.

A species is a schema file, not a code change — the mechanism doesn't care whether the next one has claws, fins, a shell, or feathers.

A species is a file

What differs between animals is a list: which measurements to take, which choices to record, which sizes the law protects. That list is a JSON file the server publishes, and every form is built from it. Adding red drum took no code at all — and it's deliberately awkward, with a slot limit instead of a minimum and no moult stage at all.

The same rules everywhere

The five-inch minimum and the egg-bearing-female rule are written once, enforced identically on the server, in the browser, and on the phone. A device with its own copy would eventually tell someone they may keep an animal the server then refuses — after they've already killed it.

SCDNR already runs the harder case

The marine game fish programme already covers 46 species across 20 families, tagged largely by volunteers, with the same tag-release-recapture shape and the same reward problem — the same programme, a different list.

Harvest tagging is a different shape

Deer isn't mark-recapture: one party, one event, compliance rather than reward. South Carolina already requires electronic reporting through SC Game Check, which issues a confirmation number a processor must trust a database to validate. A one-time tag anyone can check without asking us does the same job, without the database — modelled here, deliberately not built.

The same section appears on the page a crabber sees: a programme that oversells its evidence is worse than one that has less of it.

When a record was made, and that it has not changed
Each tagging and each report is a Bitcoin transaction. Once a block carries it the record cannot be backdated or edited — not by a crabber, not by SCDNR, not by us.
That the reporter held the tag
The reward unlocks only with the key printed on the tag itself.
That each reward is paid once
It is a single coin. A duplicate claim fails.
Who stands behind a record
Every record is signed by whoever made it, and anyone reading the public dataset can re-derive the signing key and check it themselves.
Where anyone actually was
Positions come from the phone that scanned the tag, taken on trust. The device's own reported accuracy is recorded alongside every fix. This is an attestation, nothing more.
That a crab was really released
Also taken on trust. The held bonus makes a false claim unprofitable, not impossible; a second sighting corroborates it. That isn't evidence.
  • Create a batch and print the sheet on waterproof tag stock — ids and codes are generated together, nothing spent yet.
  • Fund one wallet with the pilot's budget.
  • In the field, scan a tag, record the crab as usual, and arm it — the reward locks to that tag at that moment.
  • A crabber scans it, reports the catch, and is paid. You get a position fix taken where they stood, not where they remembered standing.
  • Export the dataset whenever you like; check any row against the chain.
